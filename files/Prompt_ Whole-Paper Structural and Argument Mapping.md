# Whole-Paper Structural and Argument Map

Analyze the scholarly paper provided below using a **text-based tree diagram**. The purpose is to create a reverse outline that reveals both:

1. **the linear order of the paper's paragraphs**, and
2. **the logical and rhetorical relationships among those paragraphs**.

The analysis should show not merely what each paragraph is *about*, but **what each paragraph is doing in the paper's reasoning** and what other paragraph, claim, section, or larger point it supports.

Do not rewrite, edit, or evaluate the prose unless specifically instructed. First reconstruct the paper's existing structure as faithfully as possible.

## 1. Unit of Analysis

Treat each paragraph as the primary unit of analysis.

Number paragraphs consecutively throughout the paper:

`¶1`, `¶2`, `¶3`, etc.

Preserve the paper's existing section and subsection headings and indicate which paragraphs belong to each section.

For each paragraph, assign:

**¶# FUNCTION — CONTENT GLOSS**

For example:

```text
¶7  C1 — Standardized screening improves identification of social isolation
```

The **function** describes what the paragraph does.

The **content gloss** states, in one concise phrase or sentence, the specific proposition, issue, evidence, or other content through which it performs that function.

The content gloss should capture the paragraph's **point or contribution**, not merely its topic.

Prefer:

```text
¶7  C1 — Social isolation frequently goes undetected without routine screening
```

rather than:

```text
¶7  C1 — Social isolation
```

## 2. Functional Notation

Use the following codes as appropriate.

| Code | Function | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Issue / problem / question | What problem, gap, tension, or question needs to be addressed? |
| **P** | Point / thesis / conclusion | What does the author want the reader to conclude or accept? |
| **C** | Supporting claim | What reason or proposition supports a larger point? |
| **E** | Evidence | What evidence, data, findings, examples, or observations support a claim? |
| **A** | Analysis / interpretation | What does the evidence mean, and how does it support the argument? |
| **B** | Background / context | What information does the reader need in order to understand the argument? |
| **L** | Literature / existing knowledge | What does previous scholarship or existing evidence establish? |
| **M** | Method / procedure | How was something done, measured, implemented, or determined? |
| **R** | Response / qualification | What limitation, alternative explanation, objection, qualification, or counterargument is addressed? |
| **X** | Transition / link | How does one part of the argument connect to another? |

Use additional functional labels only when genuinely necessary, and define any additional label before using it.

## 3. Identify Hierarchical Relationships

Do not produce only a sequential list of paragraphs.

Use a **tree structure** to represent logical dependency and argumentative hierarchy.

For example:

```text
SECTION 2 — Need for Intervention

¶6  I — Current practice does not systematically identify social isolation
│
└── ¶7  P — A standardized screening process is needed
    │
    ├── ¶8  C1 — Social isolation is associated with important health outcomes
    │   ├── ¶9  E1 — Research links social isolation with depression
    │   └── ¶10 E2 — Research links social isolation with increased mortality
    │
    ├── ¶11 C2 — Existing identification practices are inconsistent
    │   └── ¶12 E3 — Current-state review demonstrates inconsistent documentation
    │
    └── ¶13 R — Screening alone is insufficient without a follow-up process
```

Indentation and branches should indicate **support, development, qualification, or dependency**, not merely chronological sequence.

Paragraph numbers preserve the actual order of the paper.

## 4. Distinguish Sequence from Logical Structure

The paper is linear, but its argument is hierarchical.

A paragraph appearing immediately after another paragraph does not necessarily support that paragraph.

Determine relationships by asking:

- What larger proposition does this paragraph help establish?
- What claim does this evidence support?
- What evidence does this analysis interpret?
- What issue does this point answer?
- Is this paragraph subordinate to another paragraph or parallel to it?
- Does this paragraph begin a new branch of the argument?

Represent the resulting relationships in the tree.

## 5. Number Related Claims When Helpful

Use numbering to make argumentative relationships visible:

`C1`, `C2`, `C3`

`E1`, `E2`, `E3`

`A1`, `A2`, etc.

Where useful, use hierarchical numbering:

```text
P
├── C1
│   ├── C1.1
│   │   └── E1.1
│   └── C1.2
│       └── E1.2
└── C2
    └── E2
```

Do not introduce elaborate numbering when the simpler notation communicates the relationship adequately.

## 6. Analyze Sections as Argumentative Units

For each major section or subsection, identify its **section-level function** and **section-level point**.

Ask:

- Why does this section exist?
- What contribution does it make to the paper?
- What proposition, conclusion, or understanding should the reader have after completing the section?
- How do its paragraphs collectively establish that contribution?

Begin each section analysis with a concise description such as:

```text
SECTION 3 — Evidence for the Practice Change
Function: Establishes that the proposed intervention is supported by existing evidence.
```

Then provide the paragraph tree.

If a section is primarily descriptive, methodological, or procedural rather than argumentative, represent that accurately rather than forcing it into a claim-evidence structure.

## 7. Analyze the Paper at the Highest Level

After mapping individual sections, create a **whole-paper tree** in which the major nodes represent sections rather than paragraphs.

For example:

```text
PAPER
│
├── SECTION 1  I — Establishes the practice problem
│
├── SECTION 2  L — Establishes what existing evidence shows
│
├── SECTION 3  P/C1 — Establishes the rationale for the proposed intervention
│
├── SECTION 4  M — Explains how the intervention was implemented and evaluated
│
├── SECTION 5  E/A — Presents and interprets project outcomes
│
└── SECTION 6  P — Synthesizes findings and implications
```

Where the relationships are genuinely argumentative, show that hierarchy explicitly:

```text
PAPER THESIS / CENTRAL POINT
│
├── SECTION 1 — Establishes the problem requiring a response
│
├── SECTION 2 — Establishes the evidentiary basis for the response
│   ├── Claim 1
│   ├── Claim 2
│   └── Claim 3
│
├── SECTION 3 — Translates the evidence into the proposed intervention
│
├── SECTION 4 — Tests or implements the intervention
│
└── SECTION 5 — Establishes what follows from the results
```

Choose the representation that best fits the actual genre and logic of the paper.

## 8. Do Not Force Every Paragraph into an Argument Template

Not every paragraph requires Issue, Claim, Evidence, Analysis, and Point.

For example, a methods section might appropriately contain:

```text
¶31 M — Describes participant eligibility criteria
├── ¶32 M — Describes recruitment
├── ¶33 M — Describes the intervention
└── ¶34 M — Describes outcome measurement
```

A literature section might contain:

```text
¶12 L — Evidence generally supports routine screening
│
├── ¶13 E1 — Study A found...
├── ¶14 E2 — Study B found...
├── ¶15 R  — Evidence is weaker in one population
└── ¶16 A  — Collectively, the literature suggests...
```

Represent the structure that actually exists.

## 9. Identify Uncertain Relationships Explicitly

Do not invent logical relationships simply to make the tree neat.

When a paragraph's function or relationship is ambiguous, mark it with `?`:

```text
¶18  E? — Reports prevalence estimates, but its relationship to the surrounding claim is unclear
```

or:

```text
├── ? ¶22 C? — Appears to introduce a new claim whose relationship to the section point is unclear
```

Explain the ambiguity in the structural observations.

An untidy map that accurately represents the paper is preferable to an elegant map that imposes reasoning not present in the text.

## 10. Distinguish Content from Function

Do not confuse what a paragraph discusses with what it accomplishes.

For example:

```text
WEAK:
¶8 — Loneliness screening

BETTER:
¶8 C1 — Routine loneliness screening increases systematic identification of at-risk clients
```

Likewise:

```text
WEAK:
¶14 — Smith et al. (2024)

BETTER:
¶14 E1 — Smith et al. provide evidence that routine screening increases identification rates
```

The content gloss should ordinarily express a **proposition** when the paragraph itself advances a proposition.

## 11. Preserve Important Cross-Paragraph Relationships

Some argumentative units extend across several paragraphs. Show this explicitly.

For example:

```text
¶10 C1 — Screening is warranted because social isolation has clinically important consequences
│
├── ¶11 E1 — Evidence concerning depression
│   └── ¶12 A1 — Explains the clinical significance of the association
│
├── ¶13 E2 — Evidence concerning cardiovascular outcomes
│   └── ¶14 A2 — Explains the significance of these findings
│
└── ¶15 P1 — Synthesizes the evidence into a rationale for screening
```

This is preferable to treating ¶10–¶15 as six independent paragraphs.

## 12. Diagnostic Analysis

After constructing the map, examine the structure without assuming that every unusual pattern is a defect.

Identify patterns worth reviewing, including:

- paragraphs whose function is unclear;
- paragraphs with no identifiable contribution to the section;
- claims that appear unsupported;
- evidence whose target claim is unclear;
- long sequences of evidence with little visible analysis;
- background or literature review that does not clearly connect to the paper's argument;
- multiple paragraphs that appear to make the same contribution;
- transitions between branches of the argument that are difficult to reconstruct;
- section points that remain implicit;
- paragraphs that appear to contain more than one major argumentative function;
- sections whose relationship to the paper's central purpose is unclear;
- conclusions that do not clearly follow from the preceding structure;
- important claims that receive disproportionately little development;
- minor claims that receive disproportionately extensive development.

Treat these as **diagnostic observations**, not automatic writing problems.

Do not recommend revisions unless explicitly asked.

## 13. Output Format

Produce the analysis in five parts.

### Part 1 — Paper-Level Orientation

Briefly identify:

- the apparent genre or type of scholarly paper;
- its central purpose or thesis;
- its major sections;
- the overall argumentative or explanatory strategy.

Keep this concise.

### Part 2 — Section-by-Section Paragraph Maps

For every section, provide:

**Section [number/title]**

**Section function:** [one concise statement]

**Section point:** [what this section establishes, if identifiable]

```text
[paragraph-level tree]
```

Then provide a short **Structural note** explaining any important features or ambiguities.

### Part 3 — Whole-Paper Tree

Create a compressed tree showing how the major sections contribute to the paper as a whole:

```text
PAPER — [central purpose/thesis]
│
├── SECTION 1 — [function/contribution]
├── SECTION 2 — [function/contribution]
│   ├── [major argumentative branch]
│   └── [major argumentative branch]
├── SECTION 3 — [function/contribution]
└── SECTION 4 — [function/contribution]
```

This should reveal the **architecture of the entire paper**, not merely reproduce the table of contents.

### Part 4 — Structural Signature

Provide a highly compressed representation of the paper's progression.

For example:

```text
Problem → Background → Literature → Rationale
→ Intervention → Results → Interpretation → Implications
```

Where useful, also provide an argumentative version:

```text
I → {C1, C2, C3} → P → M → E → A → P
```

### Part 5 — Diagnostic Observations

Identify the **3–10 most important structural features worth examining**.

For each observation, cite the relevant paragraph numbers and explain the structural issue neutrally.

Do not rewrite the paper or recommend specific revisions unless asked.

## Governing Principle

The objective is not to determine whether the paper conforms to a predetermined paragraph formula.

The objective is to answer:

> **Can a reader reconstruct what each paragraph contributes, what it supports, what supports it, and how those relationships collectively produce the paper's larger argument?**

The resulting tree should function simultaneously as a **reverse outline, lightweight argument map, and structural diagnostic**.
